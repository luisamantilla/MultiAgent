This could have several explanations. The first is that pro-DCs were
not a ‘pure’ population and that there were still distinct populations
within what we classed as pro-DCs, which our panel of tested markers
could not discriminate. Alternatively, our pro-DC population was, in
fact, ‘homogeneous’ and single cells can give rise to different outcomes
on a cell-to-cell basis. For this latter option, stochastic cell-intrinsic
cues, or signals from neighboring cells in culture, or both, could
determine the cell’s fate. Which of the above scenarios accounts for the
phenomenon is not clear, but is currently under investigation. More-
over, whether such a process applies in vivo will also require further
investigation.

In an attempt to find an upstream precursor stage to pro-DCs,
where all singles cell putatively generated all DC subtypes, we cultured
single cells of still earlier multipotent progenitors (MPPs) that are
capable of generating all hematopoietic lineages. MPPs are the major
cell type within lineage-cKit+Sca-1+ BM cells. Although there was a
greater proportion of clones that generated all three DC subtypes, to
our surprise there were still single cells that could produce only one or
only two subtypes. In particular, the percentage of pDC-only clones
produced by MPPs was larger, as was their clone size, compared to
pro-DCs. It is actually for this reason that we prefer the term ‘pro-
DCs’ to ‘CDPs’ (common DC precursor), as the term ‘common’
implies that all cells can equally produce all DC subtypes, whereas pro-
DC implies a cell that is ‘primed’ to a particular subtype.

This is reminiscent of the in vivo progenitor stages of
hematopoiesis, rather than an all-or-nothing event at a fixed stage, the
classic branching models of hematopoiesis are not informative
enough. For example, our data suggest that DC development in
general can branch at many stages along the hematopoietic pathway
with a more defined branch at the pro-DC stage. However, if one
looks at pDC-only precursors, for example, these can branch off with a
greater probability earlier in hematopoiesis, such as at the MPP stage,
with some potential still remaining in downstream steps, such as the
pro-DC step. For want of a better term, I will call this graded
commitment—that is, each division down certain pathways has a
probability of a committed precursor for a cell type’s ‘branching off’
(Figure 4), and this may increase (or decrease) on a population level
depending on the lineage. Thus, perhaps part way down the haemo-
topoietic pathway a single cell initiates the ‘all DC program’, which can
then potentially switch to either the ‘pDC program’, ‘CD8+ and cDC
program’, ‘CD8+ DC program’ and so on.

The molecular mechanisms that would guide such fates may
include the numerous transcription factors that have been described
that differentiate unique DC subtype development (reviewed in Short-
man and Naik7). Their regulation may be stochastic within the cell, or
depend on external cytokine or cell-associated signals. The Flt3/FL axis
may be an ‘instructive’ mediator in the ‘all DC’ program with evidence
that enforced expression of committed Flt3 megakaryocyte/erythroid
progenitors could direct DC development.23,25,26 Another key player
that could support this model was published recently using human
DCs that develop in NOD-scid severe combined immunodeficiency mice,
which suggested the pDC and cDC development program is inde-
pendent of the conventional myeloid and lymphoid pathways. The
authors found that, no matter whether the DCs were CMP- or
CLP derived, they had near identical gene expression profiles.23,27

Figure 4 The ‘graded’ commitment model of dendritic cell (DC)
development. There does not appear to be defined stages where every
cell within a population can, or cannot make a particular DC subtype. Rather,
there is a gradation of probability of getting an ‘all DC’ clone versus a
plasmacytoid cell (pDC)-only or conventional DCs (cDC)-only clone from a

development on the single cell level. The molecular mechanisms that dictate
